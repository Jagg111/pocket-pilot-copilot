## Getting started

_New here? Start with these. They explain what you do as a pilot and find your way round the airport screen._

### Welcome aboard

_What you do as a pilot_

Pocket Pilots is a flying career over the real world: real terrain, real airports and today’s real weather, shared with every other pilot playing.

You choose the work; your captain flies it. Pick a job from an airport’s board, take off, and the plane flies itself there in real time while you watch, look around, take photos or put the phone away.


#### How a career goes
- Start at an airport of your choice with a Skylark and $2,500.
- Fly jobs to earn money, reputation and logbook hours.
- Take checkrides for licenses that open bigger planes, then buy them.
- Land somewhere new for a passport stamp and an explorer’s bonus.
- Join or found a company to fly together, hold airports and build them up.

> **Tip:** You fly one plane at a time, and every plane stays where it lands. Plan where you finish!


### Your first flight

_From picking where to start to the first landing_


#### Pick where to start
Choose one of the suggested places (San Diego, San Francisco, Seattle, Swiss Alps, New York and Queenstown) or open the map and pick any airport with a runway of 300 m or more. You start there with a Skylark in your own colours and $2,500. It’s your home airport from then on, but it doesn’t tie you down. Places where it’s daylight now come first. On a phone, “Get the app” there puts Pocket Pilots on your Home Screen, free and with no App Store: install first, then pick where to start in the app. On a computer, the QR code in the corner opens it on your phone.


#### Your first solo
Your first flight is your first solo: take off from home, fly a lap of the pattern and land back where you started, about four minutes. Your captain flies it; you watch. A card at the foot of the screen counts down to the touchdown (“Touchdown in 3:40”) and says what’s happening at each step: the take-off, the climb out, the turn back (or crosswind, downwind and base), final, short final, the flare and the touchdown, the camera picking a view for each. It pays $2,000 and earns you your wings. After it, the Jobs tab fills with real work.


#### Sign in after your first flight
Picking a place starts your career there at once, with no account: fly your first solo and see what it’s like. Picking it agrees to the terms, as the note under the places says. Then sign in with Apple or Google to keep it: the pay, the Skylark, the logbook, and to fly on. When you land, the result card offers the sign-in there and then, with the pay (Not now puts it off; Fly asks again). In Instagram’s or Facebook’s own browser, where signing in can fail, “Sign in in Chrome instead” (Android) takes your career along to Chrome. Till you sign in, a career does nothing that lasts beyond its flight: no jumps, purchases, check-ins, companies or new call sign, and other pilots don’t see your plane parked. One you never sign in is kept 7 days after its last flight, then it’s gone.

Signed in before, on any device? “Flying already? Sign in” on the first screen brings your career back instead (signing in after a first flight does too: that flight’s career is dropped and yours comes back). A career from before sign-ins carries on with “Sync code”. Now and then the server can’t start a career without a sign-in (offline, or too many from one place lately): then the sign-in comes first, the place you picked showing behind it.


#### Pick a job
The Jobs tab lists the work on offer, the best paid for the time it takes first, and that one’s already picked. Each row shows where to, what it pays after fuel and maintenance, the distance and how long it takes in your plane. Tap another to pick it instead (the map button under its tag shows its way; the one in the corner shows all your plane can reach, up to 1,500 nm round). One job a flight, however many seats are spare. On the map you can also pick a sight to fly past, for a photo tip from the passengers (one that adds 80 nm or less to the way; those farther off are dimmed): the job then shows “Detour past …”; tap it to pick another, or × to fly without one.


#### Fly
Tap Fly: it says where to, the pay and how long. The captain taxis out, takes off, climbs, cruises and lands, all in real time. Look around by dragging, change the camera, take a photo, or close the app: the flight carries on.


#### Land
At the other end the result card adds it up: the pay and any bonuses, less fuel and maintenance, and new stamps or awards; what you’re working towards, moved on. Then “Next job from …” shows the board there, its best job picked.

> **Tip:** Short hops are a good start: a 20–30 nm job takes a Skylark about 15–20 minutes.


### The airport screen

_Header, tabs, Fly, the buttons up top_

Wherever you land, the airport screen is home base: your plane parked on the apron, and a sheet of everything you can do there.


#### The header
- The airport’s code and name, and your money.
- A line on the field: its type (hub, regional or airstrip), its longest runway (and whether it’s unpaved), the local time and the weather, and the holder’s colours if a company holds it.
- Tap the airport’s code (a button at the top left) or anywhere on the header for the field’s card, where you can also make it your home: the city and country it serves, the weather report in words and as pilots write it, the airport’s level and what it pays, who holds it and what that means, and how many pilots are online.
- The week’s world event, in gold, when it’s here: tap it for the World screen.


#### The tabs
Each says how many it holds.

- **Jobs:** The board: work from here
- **Hangar:** Your barn finds, then your planes: those here first
- **Shop:** Planes for sale here today
- **Company:** Your company here, who holds the airport


#### The foot
Fly takes off with the job picked, and says where to, the pay and how long. Arriving, the board’s best is picked for you; tap another to pick it. Beside Fly are the route (the captain’s call: Direct or Scenic) and the map. A flight with no job (a free flight) starts on the map: tap an airport there, or the board’s last row.


#### Up top
- **Menu:** Career, planes for sale, jumps, company, world, the wiki, Discord, options
- **Career:** Your career (a light: a checkride’s ready)
- **World:** The World screen; the green number in its corner counts the flights in the air right now (a light: the week’s event within reach, or a tier reached)
- **Jumps:** How many jumps you have; the check-in and the jump shop. When the day’s check-in is waiting, a badge says what it pays: tap to collect it
- **Photo:** At the top right of the scene (on a wide screen, by the sheet): photo mode, for a picture of your plane on the apron

Under them, the goal card: what your career’s working towards. Before the first flight, how to start; then the next license and its furthest-off requirement, a checkride that’s ready (Take it), or, till you have a plane bigger than a Skylark, a Packhorse once your CPL’s in and you can afford it. Tap it for Career › Licenses, or the Shop.

_On a phone (or a narrow window), drag the list or its handle up to see more of it, and down from its top or the handle to see your plane again; with a mouse, the wheel does the same. Drag the plane to walk round it._


### Real time

_Flights take real minutes, even with the app shut_

Every flight flies in real time: a 100 nm trip in a Skylark takes about an hour. Faster planes get there sooner, and pay is set by the distance, not the time, so faster planes earn more an hour.

The scenery and data a flight will need start downloading as soon as you pick its job or destination (and for the top two jobs on the board while you look), so they’re usually in by the time you tap Fly. Fly first files the flight plan: for a few seconds the button is greyed out and says Filing flight plan, first with how much of the scenery on the way it’s downloaded (Research), then how far the captain’s got working it out (Planning), and a map over the board draws the way as it’s worked out, the whole flight from the runway to the stop at the gate, in the wind at takeoff and the winds aloft along the way (see Weather; it stays that wind for the flight, and it’s the wind the HUD, the minimap and the towers give, without gusts: it’s flown steady). A free flight with stops is filed leg by leg, each from the stop it lands at, in the wind there, so the map draws the whole route and its time counts every leg and stop. Then the map says how long it takes and when it lands, and the button lights up with a chime and says Take off with that time and the pay after costs (a free flight’s cost): tap it to go, or × to leave it. The × on the map works while it’s still filing too: on a slow phone a long trip with stops can take minutes, and × stops it and gives you the board back (Fly files it again from the start). So the time to land is known to the second, and the flight is the same whether you watch it or not. Every kind of flight is filed this way, low and scenic too, with tours, searches, surveys, fire runs and photo passes flown in full round their sights (a search party found sends you home early, and the plan’s filed again from there). While it files and waits, Route, the map and the Hangar, Shop and Company tabs are locked (they’d change the flight); leaving the airport screen drops the plan. Switching to another app pauses it, and it carries on when you come back (after 10 minutes away it’s dropped and Fly files it again).

You don’t have to watch. Close the app or lock the phone and the flight carries on; come back and it’s where it really is, or landed, with a card saying what happened while you were away (sights you passed, the landing). While the world loads, the loading screen already shows your flight: where from and to, how far along, the time to go and when it lands (on your own clock, wherever it’s going).

The menu doesn’t pause anything. Time keeps going for everyone, so other pilots’ flights land on time too.

> **Tip:** Turn on notifications (Menu → Options) to hear when you’re near a sight to photograph, on approach and landed.

### Historical planes

_Warbirds and classics_

Historical planes are the warbirds and classics: flown today for the love of them, and the stars of every air show.

- Only historical planes fly air shows, where they stand on display with the others.
- They fly the rest of the board too, as their seats and room allow.

### Barn finds

_Old planes waiting to fly again_

Here and there an airport hides a barn find: a classic plane, forgotten for decades, waiting to be restored. Such an airport shows a ? after its code: on the map once you’re zoomed in to its runway, at the top of its airport screen, and in the job rows going there.

Land there, on a job, a free flight or a jump, and a card shows what’s waiting and what restoring it takes. Restored, it’s yours to keep: a historical plane in its period colours, never sold.

Word gets round, too: once you’ve flown 5 flights, the first time you land within 500 nm of one you haven’t begun (or within your plane’s range, if that’s less), a card says where it is, with a button to see it on the map. Each find’s card comes once.


#### Restoring one
A restoration costs $250,000 to $100M, in four stages. Each is paid for at the find’s airport, then takes real time (the same for everyone, on our server’s clock), even with the app shut. Between stages it waits for you: carry on whenever you’re back there.

| Stage | Of the cost and the time |
|---|---|
| 1. Recovery | 15% |
| 2. Airframe | 40% |
| 3. Engine | 25% |
| 4. Finish | 20% |

- Recovery: hauled out, taken apart and looked over.
- Airframe: stripped to bare metal, rebuilt and primed.
- Engine: the engine overhauled on a stand.
- Finish: painted in its period colours and test-run.

- You need the plane’s license to start; after that, only the money for each stage.
- From the first stage on it’s in your Hangar and on the Career screen: how far it’s got, the time left, and the next stage to pay for. Elsewhere, Jump takes you and your plane there.
- Only you see your restoration: on its airport’s apron it stands as far as it’s got, as found, then bare metal and primer, then in its colours. Other pilots see an ordinary airport.
- Once begun, the ? is gone for you; everyone else still sees it.
- When the last stage is done the plane is parked at the find’s airport, ready to fly, with a card to say so (and a notification, if they’re on).
- One of each find per career. Restored, it can’t be sold, painted or lost.


> **Tip:** Where are they? Look for a ? on the map.


### Buying and selling

_The shop, stock, selling back_


#### The shop
Each airport’s Shop tab has one or two types for sale today, in their own random colours. Only planes that fit its longest runway are sold there, and every pilot sees the same stock. It changes every day (at midnight UTC); the top of the list says how long till the new stock.

To buy you need the plane’s license and the money. Tap a plane for its page, then Buy, then Confirm. The new plane is the one you fly from then on; the one you had stays parked where it is.

Check the runway before you buy: each Shop row starts with the runway the plane needs and this airport’s (in red when it’s too short). The board only offers jobs a plane’s runway can handle, so a plane that needs more than your home field has finds little to fly there. A plane you can’t buy yet says which license it needs: tap that for Career › Licenses.

## Jobs and pay

_Jobs are how you earn. Every airport has a board of them, sized for the planes parked there._

### The job board

_What’s on offer and when it changes_

Every airport has a board of jobs, made for the planes you have parked there: their range, seats, cargo room and the runways they need. A new board comes every 30 minutes, and the header says how long until the next.

Each row shows the job’s tag; where it goes (the airport’s code and city, and its size: hub, regional or strip, the name cut short if it’s long) and what it pays after fuel and maintenance; what it is and its load, the distance and how long it takes in your plane (flown low if the route’s Scenic). Then chips for anything else to know:

- **Red:** Limits and penalties: a medevac’s deadline, poor weather there without an instrument rating
- **Gold:** The week’s world event
- **Orange:** The season: ski trips, beach holidays, the northern lights
- **Purple:** A big charter, and how many are left
- **Green:** Bonuses: short fields, all flown, network legs, IFR in poor weather
- **Blue:** Who and what: a photo on the way, contracts, supplies, how much closer it takes you

The jobs your plane can take come first, the best paid for the time they take first, and that one’s picked when you arrive. The rest are folded under one row (“4 more the Skylark can’t take”): tap it to see them, each with a red line saying why not. The last row starts a free flight.


#### Sort and filter
Over the list, two chips. Sort orders it by the best pay an hour, the most pay or the shortest flight. The filter shows only the jobs you want: Towards event, the week’s event (its own jobs first, then any job that takes you closer to its region, from anywhere; inside the region, just its own), new airports for the passport, or your company’s work. Jobs towards the event say how much closer they take you. The rest fold under a row of their own (“12 other jobs”). Both stay as you left them, on this device, the next time you open the game. To find jobs going somewhere in particular, look on the map: it shows where each job goes.

- One job a flight, and a job is flown once: a job you’ve done won’t come back.
- Hubs, regionals and airstrips each offer their own kind of work (see Hubs, regionals and airstrips); each airport level adds 5% to its pay.
- Jobs are the same for everyone at an airport, so you may see other pilots flying them too.
- Tap a job to pick it, then the map button under its tag for the way it will be flown. On the map, a job’s card flies it: Fly it.
- Tap a row’s tag for what that kind of job is. At the foot of the board, “What the job types mean” explains them all.


#### What turns up
- Ordinary work: passengers, freight, mail.
- Premium work: VIPs, precious cargo and executive charters (a Commercial license).
- Urgent work: medevac deadlines and fresh goods.
- Tours of sights within 50 nm, searches, surveys and fires near sights (light planes), and crop dusting at small fields with farmland near.
- Seasonal work, the week’s event, big charters.
- Company work at your company’s bases, supplies and building materials, contracts and network legs. It counts towards a pilot’s company jobs (the ones that open the treasury) when it’s delivered in a company plane, whatever the job, or when it’s one of the company’s own jobs (a base job, supply run, network leg or contract), even in your own plane.


### Kinds of job

_Every tag on the board_

The tag on each row says what kind of work it is: red for urgent work, amber for premium, green for tours and surveys, blue for everyday work. Each pays a passenger charter’s rate, times:

- **PAX:** Passengers: fly them there. Set the route to Scenic, low for the view, and they tip more. Pays ×1.
- **CARGO:** Cargo: freight by weight. Needs the cargo room. Pays ×0.9.
- **MAIL:** Mail: a steady sack of post to the next town. Pays ×1.
- **MED:** Medevac: land within the planned flight time and 20 minutes from take-off (a headwind uses some). In time pays more, late less. Pays ×1.5.
- **FRESH:** Fresh goods: flowers, fish or fruit that pay extra for speed. Pays ×1.3.
- **GROUP:** Group: a band, crew or squad travelling together with their kit, so it needs every seat and room for the gear. Pays ×1.3.
- **VIP:** VIP charter: well-paid passengers. Needs a commercial license. Pays ×1.8.
- **EXEC:** Executive jet charter, 1,000 nm and up, 12 aboard at most. Jets and a commercial license. Pays ×2.2.
- **ART:** Precious cargo: art, antiques and other valuables that pay double. Needs a commercial license. Pays ×2.
- **TOUR:** Scenic tour: circle a sight and come home. Take a photo for a tip. Pays ×1.
- **SEARCH:** Search and rescue: find the orange tent (a red flare at night) and tap it. Pays ×2.
- **SURVEY:** Survey: photograph 6 marks along 3 lines. Pays ×1.4.
- **FIRE:** Firefighting: three water drops on the fire. Tap DROP over it. Marked (satellite): a real wildfire NASA spotted today. Pays ×1.8.
- **SUPPLY:** Base supplies: keep a company base open. Pays ×1.
- **BUILD:** Building materials for an airport project nearby. Pays ×1.
- **SHOW:** Air show: fly a historical plane there, empty, to stand on display with the others. Only historical planes take these. Pays ×2.5.
- **CROP:** Crop dusting: four low runs over a farmer’s field. Tap SPRAY over the crops and STOP past them. Light planes only. Pays ×1.2.
- **SOLO:** Your first solo: take off, fly a lap of the pattern and land back here. Your wings, and your first pay. Pays ×1.
- **SLING:** Sling load: hooked under a helicopter on a line, flown out slowly and lowered onto a site, a platform or a roof without landing. Helicopters with a cargo hook only, by its weight. Pays ×1.1.

#### Big charters

Big charter: too many for one plane. Take as many as you have seats for; if every seat gets flown, everyone gets +50%.


### How pay works

_Distance, load, kind of work_

A job pays by the distance, the load and the kind of work: $60 for each minute a reference plane (a turboprop cruising at 180 kt true airspeed) would take, with 15 minutes for getting away and in. Tours and aerial work count half again for their circling.

The more aboard, the more it pays, though not one for one: nine passengers pay about twice what one does, 36 about four times. Cargo counts a passenger for every 100 kg, up to 150. Past 150 passengers (the heavies’ cabins) each one counts for less: 400 aboard count as about 245.

- Premium work pays more: VIPs ×1.8, precious cargo ×2, executive charters ×2.2 (see Kinds of job).
- Each airport level adds 5%.
- Company work at your bases and supply runs pay ×1.15, the week’s event jobs ×1.3 (×1.1 once its goal’s reached), building materials ×1.1.
- Seasonal trips pay extra: ski trips 25%, beach holidays 15%.
- Short fields: +10% for landing on an unpaved runway or one under 2,600 ft, marked “Short field” on the board. Not in a helicopter: it needs no runway.
- Bonuses on landing: see After landing.

> **Tip:** The pay on the board is after your plane’s fuel and maintenance, so it’s what you really make.


### After landing

_Every line on the result card_

When you land, the result card adds up the flight in groups: the job (its pay and bonuses, and in blue what it could have paid more: a photo missed, the scenic route on a passenger job, a medevac on time, a search party found), the costs, and the extras. Then what you earned in all, the job’s own figure after costs beside what the board said, and your bank; then your progress (the goal from the airport and your reputation in that work) and “Next job from …”. A job is delivered when you land where it was going (a tour, back home with the sight circled).

- **Job pay:** As on the board
- **Passengers’ photo of their sight:** Up to +25%, by its score
- **Flown low and scenic (passengers):** +10%
- **Medevac on time / late:** +30% / −30%
- **Search party found / not found:** +50% / −40%
- **Survey photos (of 6):** −20% to +40%
- **Water drops on the fire (of 3):** −20% to +40%
- **Crops covered (of the field):** −20% to +40%
- **Formation flight:** +10%
- **Night rating, landing after dark:** +25%, up to $5,000
- **Instrument rating, poor weather there:** +20%, up to $5,000
- **Poor weather, no instrument rating:** −20%, up to $5,000 (not tours)
- **Reputation in that kind of work:** +2% a level, up to $5,000

Bonuses are reckoned on the job’s pay. Then come what others take, and your costs:

- **Company share, its plane:** 30% (it pays the fuel)
- **Company share, its job in your plane:** 10%
- **Service fee to an airport’s holder:** 5% at either end
- **Fuel and maintenance:** Yours, unless a company plane

And on top: a passport stamp for somewhere new (with an explorer’s bonus), achievements earned, reputation, and a word when a checkride is ready.

#### When the autopilot can’t
The captain doesn’t crash. On the rare flight where the autopilot can’t manage (a field far up a mountain, a strip down a canyon), the mode line says AUTO ERR and the captain takes it by hand: up out of the hills, or, near the field, a fade to black and back on a short final, landing and paid like any other.


### Low and scenic

_The route: Direct or Scenic_

The Route chip beside Fly is the captain’s call for the cruise: Direct flies straight there at the plane’s usual height and speed; Scenic flies lower, for the view.

- Scenic cruises about 2,500 ft above the field (6,000 ft in a jet) at a sightseeing pace, 85% of cruise speed, and never faster than 250 kt indicated (IAS) down low: a light plane’s trip takes about 15% longer, a jet’s or a turboprop’s often a third longer or more (the Scenic button says how much for the plane and trip).
- Passengers tip 10% for it (cargo doesn’t care, and the chip says NO TIP when Scenic is set on a cargo job): there’s no flying low by hand, so to earn the tip, set the Route chip to Scenic before you tap Fly, on a passenger job.
- A medevac is always flown direct, whatever the chip is set to (it says MEDEVAC DIR): no tip, and its time to land in is the direct flight’s.
- It takes longer: about 7–16% in a propeller plane, about half again in a jet. Job rows show the scenic times when it’s on.
- You see more: sights on the way are called out as you pass them.

_Tours fly the same either way._


### Big charters

_Groups too big for one plane_

Some jobs are too big for one plane: a choir, a conference, a whole cruise ship. They’re on the airline board, so they show at hubs (4 to 6 of them) and at regionals with airline service (1 or 2), where the runway is long enough for a Skyliner.

- Take as many seats as your plane holds. The rest stay on the board for other pilots, but only until the board changes (every 30 minutes), so a group has to take all its seats by then.
- Your share pays like a job its size, when you land.
- The full-flight bonus: if every seat is flown, everyone who flew a share gets +50% of their share’s pay on top, paid once the last plane lands (it shows the next time you’re online). The charter keeps count of its seats for 12 hours after the last ones were taken or landed. A share still not landed by then, or cancelled, loses the bonus for everyone.
- A plane that seats the whole group on its own flies it as one job, with no bonus.

> **Tip:** Big charters are best with friends: a company can fill one between its pilots.


### Seasons and night

_Ski trips, beaches, city lights_

Boards follow the season where the airport is (south of the equator the other way round) and the time of day there.

- Winter: ski trips up to mountain airports (over 1,200 m), +25%.
- Summer: beach holidays to the warm coasts, +15%.
- Nights the aurora’s out near an airport (it’s real, so any season it’s dark, either hemisphere, further from the poles when it’s strong: see Weather): northern lights tours, +50%.
- Summer and autumn: fires in dry country.
- Spring, summer and autumn (all year in the tropics): crop dusting.


#### At night
After dark, tours and photos are only of the sights that light up (city lights tours), and there are no surveys, fires or crop dusting. Landing after dark builds towards the Night rating, which then pays +25% (up to $5,000 a flight).

## Tours and aerial work

_Flying that isn’t just A to B: show passengers the sights, find lost hikers, photograph the land and fight fires._

### Scenic tours

_Circle a sight and come home_

A tour takes sightseers round a sight within 50 nm of the airport and back: a mountain, a bridge, a city skyline. The captain circles it at its own height and flies home; it’s delivered when you land back with the sight circled.

- Light planes only: jets are too fast to sightsee.
- The sight sets a height to circle at; a plane that can’t climb 1,500 ft above it can’t take the tour.
- Tours count half again for the circling.
- The passengers want a photo of their sight: a good one tips up to +25%.
- A plane with no seats behind the pilot (a fighter) flies its tours alone, as photo flights: the photo is the job.


### Search and rescue

_Find the lost party, tap them_

A search flies a square spiral over the area where a party is lost. Look out for their orange tent and smoke (at night, a red flare over a campfire) and tap right on it when you see it. You’ll spot it from about 7 km if nothing’s in the way.

Once they’re found the plane heads straight home: the rest of the search isn’t flown, and the find counts even if it’s before the pattern starts. It’s passed on at once, so it counts whichever of your devices lands the flight.

A helicopter searches low, about 1,000 ft over the ground, and once you’ve found them it goes in: a hover over them, the rescuer lowered on the hoist and both winched back up, then home.

_Every landing is checked: a find only counts on a flight long enough, in time and miles, to have been out to the search area and back. If it isn’t, the card says “Find not counted” and why._

- **Found them:** +50%
- **Searched, not found:** −40%

Searches pay ×2 and need a light plane. Finding a party earns extra reputation and the Guardian Angel award.


### Surveys

_Photograph 6 marks along 3 lines_

A survey flies three lines, 8 km long and 1.5 km apart, back and forth over the land, with six marks to photograph along them. Take a photo with the camera button as you pass each mark (within 1.5 km of it): the HUD says how far the next one is, and the camera button flashes while one is in reach. A mark flown past without a photo is missed, and the next one comes up.

Each mark photographed adds to the pay: none takes 20% off, all six add 40%. Surveys pay ×1.4, by day only, in light planes.


### Fire bombing

_Three water drops on a fire_

The captain flies three runs over the fire, each down low as tankers drop: from the circling height to about 200 ft over the ground ahead by the time you can drop, and climbing away past it. Tap the red DROP button as you pass over it. The button waits, dim with the distance to go, till you’re within 450 m of the fire (a tap before then keeps the water), then lights up and pulses DROP NOW: a drop then is always a direct hit. A helicopter’s button stays dim while its bucket’s empty. Each hit adds to the pay, from −20% with none to +40% with all three.

Needs a plane that carries 400 kg of water or more, and not a jet: the Dragonfly, the Bushmaster, the Packhorse, the Puffin, the Monarch, the Shire, the Kingfisher, the Goldfinch, the Petrel, the Stork or the Kite. Fires pay ×1.8.

A helicopter with a cargo hook fights fires with a bucket slung under it: hooked on by its stand, filled at a dip tank the fire crews have set up a couple of kilometres from the fire, and carried slowly and low over it. Tap DROP as you pass over: the empty bucket goes back to the tank for more, three times in all. Home, it’s set down beside the stand before the helicopter lands.

Rows marked (satellite) are real wildfires NASA’s satellites spotted in the last day. Where there are none, made-up fires turn up in dry country in summer and autumn.


### Crop dusting

_4 low runs over a farmer’s field_

Small fields with real farmland a few kilometres out (the fields you see from the air, from satellite land cover) post crop dusting by day in the growing season (all year in the tropics), tagged CROP. The field is a strip of crops about 900 by 300 m, outlined in yellow on the ground as you come near.

The captain flies 4 runs along it, each a swath over from the last, down to 35 ft over the crops, pulling up past the field and swinging round in a tight teardrop turn onto the next. Tap the green SPRAY button as you reach the crops and STOP once you’re past them: it pulses when it wants tapping. Spray over the crops covers the field; spray off them is wasted and counts against you, and left on well past the field it shuts off by itself.

On landing, the share of the field covered adds to the pay, from −20% with none to +40% with all of it. Crop dusting pays ×1.2 and counts towards Aerial Work.

Only light planes that can fly that low and slow take it: Sparrow, Skylark, Bushmaster, Dragonfly or Barnstormer.


### Air shows

_Historical planes on display_

An air show is a day of historical planes on display at a bigger airport (a runway of 1,000 m or more), the same day for everyone: about one such airport in 50 has one each day (UTC), and nobody knows which till the day begins.

While a show’s on within reach, a historical plane’s board now and then offers the flight there: tagged SHOW, flown empty, paying ×2.5. It counts towards Aerial Work.

At the show the apron fills with historical planes standing on display, each with its own number, for anyone who’s there or flying by. Land among them and look round.

The Thumper, the barn-find Huey, is the one historical helicopter: it flies to shows too, and sets down on a stand among the planes.

> **Tip:** Only historical planes take these: see Historical planes.


### Helicopter work

_Pads, sling loads, fires, rescues, landings out_

Helicopters are a branch of their own, off the Private Pilot license: the Helicopter Rating opens the Hummingbird and Kingfisher, then the Twin-Turbine Helicopter Rating the Goldfinch and Petrel and the Heavy-Lift Helicopter Rating the Stork. Their hours count toward the helicopter ratings, not the planes’ licenses.

They need no runway: the captain lifts off from the stand, flies about 1,000 ft over the ground (higher over the tallest towers) and sets down on the stand it’ll park on.


#### Pads
With a helicopter picked, the map shows its pads (H pins): hospitals, city heliports and rooftops, remote camps and work sites, offshore platforms in the Gulf of Mexico, the North Sea and off Brazil, and ships at anchor off the bigger ports and along coasts with no platforms (they count as offshore work too, where there are no rigs). A helicopter’s board sends it out to them and home again: medevacs (a patient picked up on the way), transfers, organs, crew changes, rig spares, ship’s stores, lodge guests and VIPs to the yacht. It sets down a while at each with the rotor turning.


#### Sling loads
A helicopter with a cargo hook (all but the Hummingbird) lifts loads on a line under it and lowers them at the site without landing: generators, fuel drums, beams, containers and cabins out to remote sites, platforms, ships and rooftops. Building projects nearby want theirs flown in this way too (tagged BUILD), and count as the cabin’s would. With a load on the line it flies slowly.


#### Landings out
Landing tours set down at a wild sight (a glacier, a canyon floor, a crater’s rim), the passengers out for a look round, then home. In winter, heli-skiing flies skiers up to a high ridge and leaves them there. The captain picks the flattest ground close in.


#### Fires and rescues
Fires are fought with a bucket filled at a dip tank by the fire, and searches end with the party winched up on the hoist: see Fire bombing and Search and rescue.


### Photos

_Photo mode on the apron, and how a photo of a sight is scored_

Take a photo any time in flight with the camera button (or P on a keyboard): save it, share it or send it as a postcard (up to 20 a day). In the iPhone and Android apps, Share sends it as a postcard: a link that shows the photo. Every photo you take is kept in Career → Album on this device (the newest 150), named for the sight in it or the nearest airport to where you took it. Each photo carries where and when you took it, as a phone camera’s do: saved to your photos (or shared, or as a postcard), it shows on their map.


#### Photo mode
On the ground, the Photo button over the airport scene (or P on a keyboard) puts the menus away and leaves your plane on the apron, other pilots’ planes round it. Drag to walk round it, pinch (or scroll) to zoom, and tap the shutter (or Space) to take it. The arrow beside the shutter puts the view back as it was; × (or Escape) brings the menus back.

In flight, the camera button opens photo mode on that moment, held still while the flight carries on behind it. Opened in the air, it closes at the touchdown so you see your landing; opened on the ground after it, it stays open when the job’s done, and the result shows once you close it.

_A photo is taken at full detail, whatever your device draws the world at: with Low graphics on, or the picture drawn coarser to keep up (Dynamic resolution), the shutter says “Developing…” for up to two seconds while the finer scenery comes in, then the world goes back as it was._


#### Photos of sights
When passengers want a photo of a sight, the camera button flashes as it comes near and a frame shows where to aim. The best photo of it is scored out of 100, and pays up to +25% of the job.

All the way there, the trip card says how far off it is (Photo in 340 nm), and a camera marks the sight on your route on the map and the minimap, ticked once you have a good photo.

_Turn the view towards it if you need to. One good photo (30 or more) is all they need: the frame and the warnings go away, and any more you take are yours (the best of them still counts)._


#### What helps
- The sight in the middle, or on a third of the frame.
- A bit of sky above it, and looking up at a tall one.
- Your own plane framing it (from the outside cameras).
- Another sight in the shot too.
- Golden light at sunrise and sunset, or a city lit up at night.


#### What hurts
- Off to the side, too small, or too close.
- Something in the way: a hill (your own plane doesn’t count: the passengers shoot from the cabin).
- A tilted horizon, or looking straight down.
- Too dark, or lost in cloud.

Photos for passengers count towards the Shutterbug award; a 90 or better earns Front Cover.

## Your career

_Your career grows with every flight: licenses open bigger planes, reputation pays more, and the passport and awards keep score._

### Licenses and ratings

_Checkrides and what they open_

Licenses let you buy bigger planes (any plane you own you can always fly) and open premium work; ratings add pay. When your logbook meets a license’s needs, take its checkride for its fee: from the goal card at the airport, or Career → Licenses. A light on the Career button says one is ready.

Career → Licenses puts the next one first, with how to get on with its furthest-off requirement; then what comes after; the ones you hold are badges at the foot. The ladder runs Private, Commercial, Multi-Engine, Jet, Airline Transport; the Night and Instrument ratings come up next when they’re nearer done.

Licenses count job hours: time flown on jobs you deliver (free flights don’t count; hours flown before October 27, 2026 all do). From Commercial up, they count only from the license before (a license earned before October 23, 2026 counts all your hours), so each step takes its own flying. A helicopter’s hours, night landings and flights in cloud don’t count toward the plane licenses. The Jet rating also needs time in twins: any twin, yours or your company’s. Hubs are the airports marked Hub on their card.

#### Student Pilot · SPL
Sparrow and Skylark · local jobs and tours
- **Checkride:** Free: every pilot starts with it

#### Private Pilot · PPL
Bushmaster · Swallow · Dragonfly
- **After:** SPL
- **Flights:** 5
- **Job hours:** 2
- **Airports landed at:** 3
- **Checkride:** $1,500

#### Night Rating · NIGHT
Jobs pay 25% more landing after dark (up to $5,000)
- **After:** PPL
- **Landings after dark:** 3
- **Checkride:** $1,000

#### Instrument Rating · IFR
Jobs pay 20% more landing in bad weather (up to $5,000)
- **After:** PPL
- **Flights in cloud or rain:** 3
- **Job hours:** 10
- **Checkride:** $5,000

A flight counts towards it if, anywhere in the air, it was inside cloud (any cloud you can see the plane fly through, a broken or overcast layer, or among towering shower and storm clouds), in rain, drizzle, snow or showers, near a thunderstorm, or low down in fog, mist or haze with visibility under 5 km. Each flight counts once, when it lands, so back and forth through one storm is still one of the 3. A note says so the moment a flight logs it.

A flight flown on while the game was closed counts too: on landing, the airport reports along the way are checked, and rain, snow, showers, a thunderstorm, fog, visibility under 5 km or a broken or overcast layer under 5,000 ft reported within 30 km of where it flew logs it.

_“IMC” (instrument meteorological conditions) is pilots’ shorthand for weather like that, flown on instruments rather than by looking out. “IMC there” on a job means poor weather where it lands: it pays 20% more with this rating, and 20% less without it (“−20% without IFR”; tours excepted). Flying one is a good way to log a flight, if the weather’s still there when you arrive._

### Passport

_Stamps and the explorer’s bonus_

Your passport keeps a stamp for every airport you’ve landed at and every country. Somewhere new pays an explorer’s bonus on landing:

- **A new airport:** $250
- **A new country:** $5,000

Airports you’ve landed at show green on the map, and the names of the countries you’ve landed in are underlined, on the map and on an airport’s card and details. ## Flying

_The captain flies every trip for you, in real time, through real terrain and weather. You choose where, and watch._

### The captain

_Who flies the plane, and how_

Your captain flies every trip, from the takeoff roll to parking, like a real crew with an autopilot. You choose the work, the plane and the captain’s call (the route: Direct or Scenic).


#### A flight’s phases
- Takeoff and climb: the plane climbs at its best rate, turning for the route; with a ridge in the way soon after takeoff it climbs gently round by the valleys, or hard straight at the ridge, as a pilot would, where that saves at least 1.5 minutes and 12% of the way; circling up over the field where hills are close. Out of a strip in a canyon it takes off the way the valley opens up (if the wind lets it lift off in time) and follows the valley out; where no way out can be flown, Fly says so (often just wait for the wind to change).
- Cruise: at the plane’s usual height (lower when the route’s Scenic), round high ground rather than over it, unless climbing a little higher saves a long way round; where only a pass gets through, as high as the plane can go. Round thunderstorms too, as crews do: 10 nm off a strong one (the map’s Weather layer red, 8 mm of rain an hour or more) and 20 nm off a severe one (dark red to purple, 20 mm or more), unless the way round is much longer, then straight through; near the fields it only keeps out of the storm itself, so it can still leave or come in the other way round it. The flight plan says how much longer it is for them (“Round storms +12m”), and the captain tells you after takeoff. Near the destination the route is planned again in finer detail, so it finds the valley that leads in.
- Descent and approach: before the descent the captain plans the whole way down to the final: the turns onto it, the height all along, and any circles to lose height, for every runway end the wind allows, picking the shortest that can be flown. Where coming straight in would mean circling to lose height among hills, or a steep dive, it looks for a way down the valleys instead (some worked out ahead of time by testing arrivals into every hilly airport). The map shows the way planned. Blown off it, the captain plans again.
- Landing: a final of about 3.2 km in a light plane, up to 6 km in a turboprop and 10 km in a jet, steeper where hills want it, and lined up on it by about 500 ft in a light plane and 1,000 ft in a turboprop or jet where the hills allow (among hills by flying the final a little steeper, rather than turning onto it low). Where no planned way can be flown, light planes fly a traffic pattern at small fields and turbines a straight-in final (10 km in a jet). Then the flare, touchdown and rollout.

The crew talk as they go (white) and so does the radio, as subtitles coloured by who’s talking (the towers amber, you blue, other pilots lilac): sights out of the window, the weather, the approach. The radio calls are spoken too (Radio voices in Options, on unless you turn them off): your captain in a male voice (or a female one, in Options), the towers in two others. A tower calls your callsign first, then its own name, and your captain reads each clearance back; after takeoff the tower hands you on to Departure, who has you climb to your cruising level on a heading or direct to the field; then you’re handed to the area’s Center (Control outside North America and Australasia), and on to the next as you cross into its area, each giving you the altimeter setting (QNH outside North America); in the cruise, now and then, the altimeter again, a level to confirm or direct to the field; at the top of descent you’re cleared down with the destination’s altimeter, then handed to its Approach (a speed to slow to, in a turboprop or jet), then its Tower, and after landing to ground. From a field with no tower you call the Center yourself once well clear, and into one Center lets you go to the field’s traffic. Like real pilots and controllers, everyone words things their own way: a hello on first calling (“good morning”), a goodbye when sent on (“good day”, “cheers”, “see ya”), and now and then in the country’s own language (“bonjour”, “namaste”, “au revoir”, “g’day”). Callsigns are spelled out in the phonetic alphabet, as on a real radio: a registration in full the first time each tower or field hears it, then shortened to its last three (“Skylark 5 sierra sierra”), as ATC does it; a call sign you chose in full first too, then by its first letter and last two (“FRESCH” → “foxtrot charlie hotel”), the ICAO way. Each call’s subtitle stays up until it’s been said, and frequencies are read “decimal”, the ICAO way (“point” in the US and Canada, as the FAA says it). Between your calls you hear the other players on your frequency, so you know who’s flying near you: coming in to the field or leaving it, each in their own voice and callsign, shortened as it would be by then (at a small field without a tower, each pilot says where they are), and en route, those the area’s Center is talking to. A busy frequency stays a murmur: at least 15 s between exchanges, and no more than four in four minutes. Their calls are captioned too, in lilac, the towers’ answers to them in amber. With nobody near, the radio is quiet. At an airport you hear its tower softly. Options › Radio chatter turns it off.

> **Tip:** The approach mode shows on the data strip: VIS for a visual approach, LOC · G/S at airline fields with an instrument landing system, RNAV · GP elsewhere. Without an instrument rating, a piston plane flies visual.

### Weather

_Real reports, rain, cloud and pay_

The weather is real: the latest reports (METARs) from airport weather stations, the same for every pilot. Between stations it’s blended from the nearest; out at sea, where nobody reports, a gentle made-up climate fills in. The airport screen shows the report itself under the conditions.

The flight screen shows the local time and conditions: temperature, sky, wind (gusts after G). Wind, cloud, rain, snow, fog and haze are drawn as reported.

In a wind over 15 kt the plane rocks and sways as you watch it near the ground (a steady wind aloft carries it smoothly), light planes most, and the wind buffets in gusts you can hear. It’s for the eye and ear only: the captain flies it just the same. In the cockpit, rain beads on the windshield and streaks away up the glass as you speed up (not in the Barnstormer’s open cockpit, which has no glass).


#### Winds aloft
Up high the wind is real too: NOAA’s GFS forecast winds at about 5,000 ft, 18,000 ft and 34,000 ft, a new run every 6 hours, the same for every pilot. Near the ground you fly in the reported surface wind; from about 1,500 m (5,000 ft) up, in the winds aloft, blended between. Jets and turboprops up at their cruise heights meet the jet streams: 100 kt or more behind you can take an hour off a long trip, and the same wind on the nose adds more. Light planes down low feel far less. Like airline dispatchers, the flight plan picks its cruise height for them on trips over 150 nm: up to 4,000 ft higher into a tailwind, or lower under a headwind (as low as half the usual height, where the hills allow) when that’s at least 3% quicker over the ground. The minimap’s corner gives the wind where the plane is, so up high it’s the wind aloft. A flight keeps the winds it was filed in, and the board’s times already count them.

> **Tip:** See them on the map: Layers › Weather › Wind shows streaks drifting with the winds aloft at the height you pick (5,000, 18,000 or 34,000 ft), coloured by speed: pale and blue light, green and yellow brisk, orange, red and pink the jet stream. The key in the corner gives the speeds in knots. It replaces the rain layer while it shows.


#### The northern lights
After dark, the aurora shines where NOAA’s space weather model says it is right now, updated every few minutes: curtains of green light 100 km up and higher, with rays and folds that drift and shimmer, red tops on a strong one. It lives round the magnetic poles: overhead in northern Norway, Iceland, Alaska and northern Canada, and over the Southern Ocean; on a strong night it shows low in the sky further south (or north). Twilight washes it out and cloud hides it; seen from up high it’s at its clearest.


#### Poor weather
Rain or snow, thunderstorms, visibility under 5 km or cloud under 3,000 ft count as poor weather. Landing in it pays +20% with an instrument rating, and 20% less without (tours excepted), $5,000 at most either way. Job rows warn “IMC there” (IMC: weather flown on instruments).


#### Instrument weather
A flight is logged towards the Instrument rating (3 flights) and the Cloud Surfer award (10) if, anywhere in the air, it was inside cloud (any cloud you can see the plane fly through, a broken or overcast layer, or among towering shower and storm clouds), in rain, drizzle, snow or showers, near a thunderstorm, or low down in fog, mist or haze with visibility under 5 km. Once a flight, counted when it lands; a note says so the moment it’s logged.

To find some: rain on the map’s Weather layer; an airport’s report (on the airport screen) with broken (BKN) or overcast (OVC) cloud; jobs marked “IMC there”; or the weather line on the flight screen.

> **Tip:** The map’s Weather layer (Layers › Weather › Rain) shows the rain and snow you’ll fly into: seen by satellites (NASA’s IMERG), the same rain the game’s sky falls from, an hour or a few old. Green is light rain, yellow and orange moderate, red and darker a thunderstorm (routes are planned round them: see The captain); snow in blues and purples. Clouds, wind and temperature come from the airports’ reports, and so does rain or snow further than 60° from the equator, where the satellites don’t see it.


### Landings

_Grades, going home, when the autopilot can’t_

Every touchdown is graded by how fast the plane was sinking. It’s for the log and bragging rights; it doesn’t change the pay.

- **Butter!:** under 100 fpm
- **Smooth:** under 200 fpm
- **Firm:** under 400 fpm
- **Hard:** under 650 fpm
- **Very hard:** 650 fpm or more


#### Turning back
Turn back to (the airport you left), at the foot of the menu, asks you to tap twice, then calls the flight off: you’re back there at once, and the job is cancelled.


#### When the autopilot can’t
The captain never crashes the plane. Where the autopilot can’t manage (about to meet the ground, or once it’s had to go round, circle down onto the final again or climb back up over the hills near the field), the mode line says AUTO ERR and the captain takes over. Within 40 km of the field, the screen fades to black and comes back with the plane on a short final, a gentle touchdown and paid as usual. Further out, the captain flies it by hand: up out of the hills and on, or round and down onto a final as steep as the hills want. A landing that would have been far too hard is a firm one. Every one is noted, so the autopilot can be taught those places. A few fields no autopilot gets into in some planes (testing arrivals into every hilly airport finds them) are special approaches, as real ones are flown into such fields: the captain flies in by hand from 20 km out, the mode line saying VISUAL · BY HAND, down the valley and round onto a short final, landing on whichever end of the runway the way in suits.


### Formation flying

_Fly with another pilot for +10%_

Leave for the same airport within 3 minutes of another pilot, in a plane about as fast as theirs (cruising within 10% of the formation leader’s), and you’re paired up; more can join the same way. Once you’re a little way up your formation joins up into a V, each plane flying across into its place: its leader (the first away) at the point, you off its right wing, the rest down both sides behind (on the ground, where they really are).

Nearing the landing it peels off: the leader first, then each down the V a few seconds apart, rolling steeply away from you to fly its own way down.

Watch one of them from the map and the whole V flies round it.

_Other planes glide between their position reports rather than jumping. One that would sit inside yours is drawn off a wing, or behind you on the runway; one about to fly or roll straight through yours (head-on down the same runway, say) is hidden till it’s gone by. Your formation stays._

If both of you land there within 15 minutes of each other, the job pays a 10% convoy bonus.

> **Tip:** Fly with friends: agree a destination, take off together.

## Getting around

_Your planes stay where they land. These are the ways to move yourself and them round the world._

### Free flights

_Fly anywhere, with stops (and a job along)_

No job you want? A free flight starts on the map: pick any airport in your plane’s range (the circle), or your own for a local flight round the field.

- Free flights pay nothing, and you pay the fuel and maintenance.
- They still earn passport stamps, explorer’s bonuses and achievements.
- Good for repositioning: flying your plane to where the work is, or to a plane you’ve left somewhere.


#### Stops on the way
A free flight can call at other airports on the way: tap an airport, then + Stop, and pick the next from there (the range circle moves to it). Up to 20 stops; the last airport you pick ends the trip, and it can be the one you started from. The bar at the top shows the route and its flying time; Undo drops the last stop.

Every leg must be in the plane’s range of the airport before it, with runway enough.

At each stop the captain lands, rolls to a stop, and the view fades to the plane lined up for the next take-off, about 3 seconds later: no airport screen between. Each landing is paid (the fuel and maintenance, any new stamp or bonus) as it happens, and the last landing’s card lists the stops. In flight, the trip card at the top counts the whole route: its time left says all legs, and it says when the plane lands at the last stop, on your own clock (lands, then its code and the time); the line under it says which leg this is and the stops to come. It all carries on with the app closed, like any flight; turning back ends the trip at the airport that leg left from.


#### A job along the way
A route can carry one job from the board here, delivered at the stop that’s its airport: pick the job on the board and tap + Stop beside its Fly button (or tap its airport on the map, then + Stop beside Fly it), and pick the rest of the route from there. Or pick your stops first and tap Fly it on a job’s airport to end the route there. Plain deliveries only: passengers, cargo, charters and the like, not tours, searches, surveys, fire runs, crop dusting or photo passes. Passengers hoping for a photo of a sight on the way come along too, but the route flies them straight there: no photo, and the pay of a direct flight. The route’s Fly button shows its pay less every leg’s fuel and upkeep. It’s paid as the plane lands at that stop, and the last landing’s card shows what it paid with the rest of the trip, its after-costs figure counting the leg that delivered it. Land somewhere off the route before then and it isn’t delivered. The whole route counts against the fatigue limit, and a medevac’s clock runs from take-off.

A full card stays up until the next stamp is due (the Jumps screen says when the new card starts); the next stamp starts a fresh card, again with a jump. The first stamp comes as a card after your first flight; after that a badge on the Jumps button says a stamp is waiting and what it pays, and a tap collects it.

Pilots Club members get a jump with every stamp too, on top of what it pays: the same cash as everyone, and a jump every day. Each stamp that gave a jump has a little bolt under its cash.


### The map

_Airports, pilots, jobs, weather_

The map button (at an airport or in flight) opens the whole world. Pinch or double-tap to zoom, drag to pan.

- Airports, each a little runway pointing the way its longest runway lies: big for hubs, smaller for regionals, a short tick for airstrips (see Hubs, regionals and airstrips). The ones you’ve landed at are green; those too short for your plane are faint.
- Your planes (green) and every other pilot in the air (yellow), even those away from the game. Your company’s pilots and its allies’ are blue, with their company tag. Zoomed out, only theirs (and a pilot you’ve picked or searched for) carry a callsign. Each is drawn as what it flies: a single, a twin prop, a jet with its engines at the tail, an airliner, a four-jet on a high wing, a jumbo, a delta, a helicopter, a tandem-rotor helicopter or a tiltrotor; the historic planes (the barn finds among them) each in a shape of its own.
- Flying, with nothing picked: where you are, and Copy for a maps app; along the bottom your trip (where to, time left, a bar of the way flown, nm to go, when it lands) and ✕ at the top back to the world; the crew’s and the radio’s calls at the top; your engines heard from the cabin while it’s kept on your plane, at half their volume (the radio as usual).
- Your route as it will be flown, and your plane’s range circle.
- Job tags with their pay: tap one to see it and take it.
- Who holds which airports, company bases, and the week’s event region.


#### Map key
Every kind of mark on the map, as it’s drawn there. The same key is on the map: Layers, then Key.


**Map key (graphic legend, entries as named in the game):**

- **◎:** Back to you (flying: and kept on your plane as it goes, lit, till you drag the map; opened in the air, the map starts on your plane like this, with the trip along the bottom and ✕ to see the world: flying on the map is the easiest on slow phones and the battery)
- **Layers:** What the map shows (lit while anything’s filtered or hidden)

Your trip’s ends and where you are always show, and so do the sights while you pick a detour. The map remembers it all, even after the game’s closed.

Your home airport has a little gold house beside its pin. The plane with a green $ shows its planes for sale today. Your home’s card has a lit gold house beside it. Live view turns round the stand you’d park on there: drag to turn and tilt, pinch to zoom right in on the planes parked by it; × goes back to the map. Your own flight carries on meanwhile, and other pilots still see it where it is. Landmarks have a Live view too, on their cards.

Tap another pilot’s plane for their card: where they’re bound, how high and fast, and Watch flight to ride along. Their way shows in yellow, dashed ahead of them: the route their own captain planned round the high ground.


### Hubs, regionals and airstrips

_Three kinds of airport, each with its own work_

Every airport is one of three types, as in the real world: hubs (the big airports airlines fly from), regionals, and airstrips. The map shows which is which, and each has its own kind of work.

#### On the map
- A hub: a big runway crossed by its second one. Its code always shows, larger.
- A regional: a smaller runway.
- An airstrip: a short tick.
- Pale olive: grass or dirt.
- Green: you’ve landed there.
- Faint: too short for the plane you’re flying.

Each pin points the way the airport’s longest runway lies. Zoomed out you see the hubs, then the regionals, and close in the airstrips fade in. A smaller field with nothing else near (an outback strip, an island’s only runway) shows as far out as the regionals, as long as there’s room round it. Antarctica’s few airfields, all airstrips, show from afar with their codes, so they’re easy to find; their runways are groomed snow and ice, pale blue on the map. Nothing grows there: no trees or grass, just ice, snow and bare dark rock. The job board names each destination’s size instead: hub, regional or strip.

#### Hubs
- Long legs and charters, where the big money is: 1 short hop, 2 regional legs, 3 long legs, 2 charters.
- Long hauls for every plane parked there (but airliners): its charters and one long leg go 60% to 90% of its range, most of them to another hub, so a plane bought for its range finds work that uses it.
- 4 to 6 airline charters (see Big charters).
- Connecting passengers off the airlines, on to the regionals and strips round it.

#### Regionals
- Commuter work: 3 short hops, 3 regional legs, 1 long leg.
- With airline service, 1 or 2 airline charters.
- A ride to the nearest hub to catch a flight, and passengers connecting on out to a strip.

#### Airstrips
- Local and bush work: 4 short hops, 2 regional legs, mostly to other strips and the nearest regional.
- Mail, fresh goods, medevacs, farm and camp supplies; no VIPs or precious cargo.
- More aerial work: a survey on 40% of boards (elsewhere 25%), a search on 50% (elsewhere 35%), near the sights; crop dusting on 60% (elsewhere 40%) where there’s farmland near.

#### Everywhere
- Short fields pay +10%: landing on grass or dirt, or on a runway under 2,600 ft (not in a helicopter, which needs no runway).
- No board goes empty: with nowhere in its usual reach, it finds work at hubs and regionals up to 400 nm off, at least 6 jobs.
- Planes with no passenger seats find at least 3 courier jobs sized to their hold.
- Airport levels (built up by everyone’s flights) add a slot and 5% pay at any type.

## Companies

_One pilot flies one plane, so anything bigger is done together. A company shares planes, bases and money between its pilots._

### Companies

_Founding, joining, leaving_

A company is a group of pilots who fly together: it owns planes any of them can fly, opens bases with work of their own, keeps a treasury and holds airports.


#### Founding one
Founding costs $50,000 of your own money. Give it a name (3 to 24 letters) and a tag of 2 to 4 letters or digits, both unique (one another company has is taken); you’re its Chief Pilot, and the treasury starts empty.

Tags used to be shared. Where two companies had the same one, the first founded keeps it, and the other’s Chief Pilot is asked on the Company screen to pick a new one, free, once.


#### Joining one
Ask a company’s Chief Pilot or a Captain for its 6-character code (only they see it) and enter it on the Company screen. You join as a First Officer. A company has up to 20 pilots. If a code gets out, the Chief Pilot can make a new one (New invite code, on the Company tab): the old code and its invite links stop working, and nobody already in the company is affected.


#### Leaving
You can leave any time you’re not flying one of its planes. If the Chief Pilot leaves, a Deputy Chief takes over, else the longest-serving Captain. The last pilot out keeps the treasury; its planes and bases are gone.


#### Where to find it
The Company tab at every airport shows your company there and who holds the airport. The Company screen (the button there, or Menu → Company) has tabs for the company, its ledger, its pilots, its fleet, its bases and its airports. The company tab ends with Lately, the company’s log: who joined and left, money put in and paid out, buys, bases, contracts and alliances, the last 60 things, newest first (scroll down for them all). Airports won and lost are in the Airports tab instead.

Each pilot and plane says where it is: in the air (where to, how long to go, and who’s flying a company plane) or the airport it’s at; for other pilots, where they last landed. Tap one to see it on the map: a flight in the air with its card (Watch it from there), or the airport. The map shows the company’s parked planes in its pilots’ blue, by name (or type), stacked where several share an airport, and an airport’s card lists them.


### Ranks

_First Officer, Captain, Deputy Chief, Chief Pilot_

Every pilot in a company flies any of its planes they hold the license for, whatever their rank. Ranks are for running the company: what each may spend and decide (see Spending the treasury).


#### Promotion
You join as a First Officer. The Chief Pilot makes Captains: on Company › Pilots, a First Officer’s card has Make Captain, and a Captain’s Make FO to take it back. No number of jobs makes a Captain: it’s the Chief’s choice, so they pick pilots they trust. Captains made by the old rule (10 company jobs) stay Captains.

Each pilot’s card also shows what they’ve put into the treasury and what their flying has brought it. The list sorts four ways: Rank; Money in (those two together, the biggest first); Last seen (pilots in the air now first, then by their last landing: who’s gone quiet); and Jobs (company jobs flown).

The Chief Pilot can also hand over the company (Make chief: type the company’s name in full to confirm; the old Chief becomes a Deputy Chief), or let a pilot go.


#### Deputy Chiefs
A Captain the Chief Pilot sets to no limit on their card is a Deputy Chief, as many as the Chief likes. A Deputy does what the Chief does, so the company needn’t pass the title round while its Chief is away: spends any amount, sells planes, closes bases, makes and unmakes Captains, pays pilots, makes and ends alliances, makes a new invite code. Only the Chief changes who’s Chief or a Deputy, lets pilots go, picks the tag and sets limits. Setting a lower limit, or Make FO, ends it. If the Chief leaves, a Deputy takes over first.


#### Spending the treasury
Each Captain can spend up to $250,000 of the treasury a day (UTC) on planes, bases, gates, supplies, contracts and building projects; anything over that is the Chief Pilot’s. The Chief Pilot can give a Captain they trust more on their card in the Pilots tab: $1M or $5M a day, or no limit, so big jets needn't wait for the Chief to be online. No limit makes them a Deputy Chief (above). Only the Chief Pilot and Deputy Chiefs sell company planes and close bases.

_The Company screen shows a Captain what they have left to spend today; the Chief sees what each Captain spent today on their card in Pilots, and every buy is in Lately. A greyed button says why when tapped._


#### Who does what
- **Everyone:** Put money in, take a Base Jump, fly company planes, hand over a plane they hold, ask the company to buy them a plane
- **Captains too:** Colours, buy planes (and answer pilots’ purchase requests), open bases and gates, buy supplies, post contracts, start projects: up to $250,000 of the treasury a day each, or the limit the Chief sets them. Name company planes, jump them to bases, release anyone’s hold
- **Deputy Chiefs too:** Spend any amount, sell planes, close bases, pay pilots, make Captains and First Officers, alliances, a new invite code
- **The Chief Pilot too:** Make or unmake the Chief and Deputies, set Captains’ limits, let pilots go, pick the tag


### Treasury and planes

_Company money and company planes_


#### Who gets what from a job
| Flown in | The job | The company gets | Fuel and maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| A company plane | Any job: yours or the company’s | 30% of the pay | The company |
| Your own plane | Company work | 10% of the pay | You |
| Your own plane | Your own job | Nothing | You |

- The share is of the job’s pay, bonuses and all. In a company plane the company pays that flight’s fuel and maintenance out of its 30%; if that isn’t enough the treasury pays the rest, and if the treasury’s short the pilot does.
- Company work is the company’s own jobs: base jobs, supply runs and network legs. Only its pilots can fly them.
- Contracts are different: anyone can fly one, and the pilot keeps all the pay (the company paid it in when it posted it).
- A company with no bases has no running costs: its planes cost fuel and maintenance only when they’re flown. Bases use supplies every day (40 kg a gate).


#### Money in
- What pilots put in (from $100), once they’ve flown 10 jobs: up to $25,000 each a day (UTC). Under “Put money in”, the Company screen shows how much more you can put in today and how long till the limit resets at 00:00 UTC.
- Its share of jobs: 30% of any job flown in a company plane, 10% of company work flown in a pilot’s own plane.
- Service fees at the airports it holds or has bases at (5% of other pilots’ jobs).
- A network flown in full (the legs’ pay again), contracts nobody flew, planes sold, half the price of a base it closes.


#### Money out
- Planes, bases, gates and supplies.
- Contracts (held until flown) and building projects.
- Fuel and maintenance for its planes.
- Pay to pilots, from the Chief Pilot: to pilots who’ve flown 10 company jobs, up to $25,000 each a day. Pay isn’t “Earned on jobs”, so it doesn’t count towards a license.


#### The ledger
The Ledger tab on the Company screen shows what the company earned in the last 24 hours: its share of flights, less its planes’ fuel and maintenance; service fees at its airports; network bonuses. A bar for each hour, its parts in colour by kind: flights yellow, service fees blue, network bonuses purple, and red below the line for an hour whose flights cost more than they made. Then the flights by pilot, the fees by airport and the bonuses by base, each headed in its colour. What pilots put in and what the company spends aren’t in it.


#### Company planes
Captains and the Chief buy them in any Shop (the For TAG button), from what that airport sells today, with the treasury’s money, not their own (a Captain up to $250,000 a day): the button is greyed while the treasury’s short of the price, or it’s more than a Captain may still spend today (tap it to see why). Only the Chief Pilot sells them. Any pilot of the right rank and license at a company plane’s airport can fly it; it moves with them. The Fleet tab says where each one is, and which pilot is flying it; tap it to see it on the map. With three or more, sort them (Bought, A–Z, Hours) and group them by type or by where they are; tap a group’s header to fold it away.

A company plane stays with the pilot who just landed it for 12 hours while they’re still at that airport, so nobody flies off in it while they’re away; the Hangar and the company’s Fleet say whose it is and for how long. It’s free again once they leave there (in another plane, or a jump) or the time’s up. A pilot who closes the game mid-flight doesn’t keep it in the air: once the flight’s time is up, it’s parked where it was going and held for them from when it landed, so a Captain or the Chief can release it.


#### Purchase requests
A First Officer (or a Captain past what they may spend today, or when the treasury is short) who wants a company plane asks for it in the Shop: Ask TAG on a plane for sale there, then Send. It waits at the top of the company’s Fleet tab (its count on the tab) for 3 days. The Chief Pilot, or a Captain within their $250,000 a day, taps Buy: the treasury buys it at the airport it was asked for, held for that pilot for 12 hours, so it’s theirs to fly when they’re there. No turns it down; its pilot can Cancel it. A Captain who asked because the treasury was short can Buy their own once it has the money. One request a pilot at a time: a new one takes the old one’s place.

Parked at another airport and nobody else’s: Jump on its row in the Fleet (a Captain’s or the Chief’s on its page), or Jump to it on the map’s card for its airport, takes you there for 1 of your own jumps. Your own plane stays where it is, and the company plane is held for you when you arrive.

Done with it sooner? “Let others fly it” on its page (or Let go in the Fleet) hands it over at once. Captains and the Chief can release anyone’s hold, from its page or the Fleet, and the company’s log says who did.

Flown in a company plane, a job gives 30% to the company, and the company pays the fuel and maintenance (if the treasury is short, the pilot pays the rest).


### Bases and supplies

_Gates, company work, Base Jumps_

A base is the company’s own gate at an airport. It brings work for the company’s pilots and needs supplies to stay open.

| Airport | Base costs |
|---|---|
| Hub | $1.5M |
| Regional | $500,000 |
| Airstrip | $150,000 |

The price goes by the airport’s type, as the map shows it (Hub, Regional or Airstrip); how busy it is (Busy, Bustling, Booming) doesn’t change it.

- **Gate 2:** $100,000
- **Gate 3:** $250,000
- **Gate 4:** $600,000
- **Bases at most:** 8


#### What a base gives
- Company jobs: 2 a gate on every board, paying ×1.15, for its pilots only.
- Network legs (see Network flows).
- Base Jumps (below): 1 a week, a hub base 2, and 1 more the week its network card fills (see Network flows).
- Its colours on the hangar and the 5% service fee, when no company holds the airport.


#### Supplies
Each gate uses 40 kg of supplies a day and holds 1,000 kg. A new base starts with 500 kg. Captains and the Chief buy more on its card at $30 a kg (from a Captain’s $250,000 a day) (500 kg at a time, or fill it up: it says what it costs first), or fly them in: when a base is under half full, supply runs for it turn up on boards nearby (paying ×1.15).

> **Tip:** A base with no supplies is closed: no company jobs, no network legs, no Base Jumps to it (nor any it adds on Monday), until it’s supplied again.


#### Base Jumps
Jumps of the company’s own (the blue bolt), only ever to one of its bases with supplies. Every Monday at 00:00 UTC each base with supplies then adds 1 to the bank, a base at a hub airport 2. The bank holds two weeks’ worth: twice what Monday adds.

- Any pilot: Base Jump here, on a base’s card in the Company screen’s Bases tab, takes you there from anywhere for 1. It asks first, saying how many the company has left (every pilot shares them) and that your own planes stay where they are.
- Captains and the Chief: Jump a company plane here, on the same card, takes a parked company plane there for its usual jumps (see Jumps), if the runway suits it and nobody holds it.
- Bases tab: what’s banked, what Monday adds and how long till then, and the most it holds. The company’s log says who used them.

_Captains open bases and buy gates and supplies within their $250,000 a day; only the Chief Pilot or a Deputy Chief closes a base, which gives back half of what it cost._


### Network flows

_Legs from your bases, +50% together, Base Jumps_

Once a day (00:00 UTC) each supplied base offers a set of network legs: 2 plus one a gate, for the company’s pilots, paying ×1.15. Half are short hops, 25 to 220 nm, for a light plane; the rest go as far as the base reaches, to airports the company’s own planes can fly to (their range and runways; not helicopters or historical planes), loaded to fill most of one of them. A company with none that reach that far gets legs anywhere in the band, loaded for the plane in the table or one that fits.

| Base | Long legs |
|---|---|
| Airstrip | 100 to 220 nm, a Packhorse’s load |
| Regional | 240 to 600 nm, a Shire’s load |
| Hub | 600 to 1,500 nm, a Zephyr’s load |

Take them before the day is out. Each leg pays +10% for every leg of the flow landed before it. If every leg has landed within 4 hours after the day ends, each leg’s pilot gets +50% and the treasury gets the legs’ whole pay again.


#### The network card
A day a base’s legs are all flown in time stamps its network card (the dots on the base in the Company screen’s Bases tab). Its 4th stamp in a week (Monday 00:00 UTC to the next) adds a Base Jump to the company’s bank, even past what the bank holds: one a base a week at most.

See a base’s legs from anywhere with Network legs today on its card in the Company screen’s Bases tab: where each goes, its load and runway, the company plane a long one is loaded for (picked from the company’s planes that fit, so it can be any of them), who has taken it and which are flown. The job itself says the plane too.

> **Tip:** Network flows are made for teamwork: split the legs between the company’s pilots through the day.


### Contracts

_Work your company posts for anyone_

Captains and the Chief can post a contract: a job from an airport to anywhere up to 1,500 nm, paying the usual rate, from the treasury (it counts towards a Captain’s $250,000 a day).

- It’s open for 24 hours, to any pilot, in any company or none.
- The pay is held from the treasury when it’s posted, and given back if nobody flies it.
- Only pilots with 10 jobs flown can take one, and contracts pay a pilot up to $25,000 a day, the same limits as a payment from the treasury.
- The company that posted it gets the influence where it lands; the pilot gets all the pay.

_Use contracts to bring traffic and influence to the airports you want to hold._


### Alliances

_No fees between friends_

Chief Pilots can ally their companies: one asks with the other’s company code (Company › Airports › Ally with a company code). The request shows there for the other company by name, and its Chief Pilot accepts or turns it down. A company has up to 3 allies.

Allies pay no service fees at each other’s airports and bases.

## The shared world

_Everyone flies in the same world at the same time. Companies compete to hold airports, and everyone works on the week’s event together._

### Other pilots

_One sky for everyone_

Everyone plays in the same world at the same time. Pilots near you fly past with a tag beside them; their planes park at the airports where they land, and stay in view for two weeks (your own planes are always yours, but others no longer see one left that long).

- The map shows every pilot in the air, even those whose app is closed: their flight carries on, like yours, and lands on time, the plane parked there whether its pilot is back yet or not.
- Tap a plane on the map for its card, then Watch flight to ride along with your own cameras (drag to look round, pinch to zoom). × ends it; your own flight carries on meanwhile. They see you: their trip card counts who’s watching, and says your callsign when you start.
- Or tap an airport, then its camera button (Live view), to watch the traffic coming and going there from above, with its tower on the radio.
- Jobs are the same for every pilot at an airport, and big charters are shared out between them.


### Holding airports

_Influence, holders and fees_

Every job a company’s pilots deliver counts as influence for that company at the airport it lands at: the job’s pay. The company with the most holds the airport. When your delivery wins an airport for your company, the landing report says so (and whom it was taken from).

The Company screen’s Airports tab starts with Won and lost, newest first: each airport your company’s pilots won, who won it and whom from, and each one another company took from yours, who took it. Then the airports it holds now.

- Weeks run from Monday, 00:00 UTC. What counts is this week plus half of last week: nothing goes back to zero on Monday. The week just ended then counts half, and the one before it drops off.
- A holder needs at least $2,000. One pilot counts for up to $250,000 at an airport a week.
- The holder takes a 5% service fee on other pilots’ jobs from or to the airport (not its own or its allies’). A flight between two held airports can pay two: the landing’s pay lines name each company and its airport.
- The airport wears the holder’s colours on its biggest hangar (its body colour, banded round in its stripe and accent), and its name shows in the airport’s header.
- On the map a held airport has a square round its pin in the holder’s colours, once you’re zoomed in far enough for runway pins.


#### Leaderboards
Every airport has a company leaderboard: every company delivering there, by influence, with who holds it, how close 2nd is and how much more your company needs to take it.

- Open it from the airport’s field card (tap its code) or its Company tab (your company’s tag, beside Shop), or from any airport’s card on the map: the podium button at its top right.
- A close race (2nd within a fifth of the leader) can change hands with a few big jobs; a safe lead (the leader has twice 2nd’s or more) takes a lot more.

The World screen (the World button) ranks companies this week, worldwide and in the country you’re in: where you’re parked, or in the air the one you’re flying to. The green number over the World button’s corner is how many flights are under way worldwide right now, the same count as the foot of the World screen.


### Airport levels and projects

_Busy airports, building_

Airports get busier with traffic. Each landing counts a point, and more for well-paid jobs (another for every $20,000); the points halve every 15 days if nobody flies there.

| Level | Name | Points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Busy | 12 |
| 2 | Bustling | 50 |
| 3 | Booming | 160 |

Each level adds a job to the board and 5% to its pay (up to level 3). Its level is on the airport’s card (tap its code at the top of the airport screen), and a company’s bases show theirs in the Company screen’s Bases tab.

_A level isn’t the airport’s type (Hub, Regional or Airstrip, in its header and on the map): that’s the real airport’s, and traffic doesn’t change it._


#### Building projects
Captains and Chiefs can start a building project at an airport, paid from the treasury. Then any pilot can fly the materials in from airports 10 to 300 nm away (paying ×1.1). Once it’s all delivered it’s built for good, for everyone to see, and the airport goes up a level.

| Project | Cost | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| Hangar | $100,000 | 6 t |
| Fuel farm | $150,000 | 8 t |
| Runway lights | $80,000 | 4 t |
| Terminal | $300,000 | 15 t |

The two add up: an airport’s level is its traffic level plus one for each project built (up to 3), so either can get it there, and a built level doesn’t fade in quiet weeks.

_One project at a time at an airport, and each only once._


### World events

_One a week, everyone together_


#### This week
_(This week’s event is live data and changes weekly. When captured: Typhoon Relief Airlift — “Food, water and shelter to the islands after the typhoon. Goal: 11,000 t, reached. Into: its jobs are on boards outside its region, up to 900 nm away, flying in from 100 nm or more. Boards inside it have none: fly other work out and come in again.”)_


#### How it works
Each week (Monday to Monday, UTC) one region of the world has an event: an airlift, a ski shuttle, a seafood rush, a festival. Its banner shows on airport screens there, and its region on the map.

- Event jobs pay ×1.3 (×1.1 once the goal’s reached). Where they are depends on the event’s way (below): Inside, on boards in the region (up to 3 a board, at least one on every board there, day or night), and one on boards within 160 nm of it, into it, when somewhere there suits your plane.
- Each week’s goal is set from its own first day: when Monday (UTC) is over, it’s 5 times what everyone delivered that day, so it’s reached about Friday whatever the region, the planes that fit it, how many come and its way. The first day counts towards it like any other, and no tier can be reached before the goal is set. It doesn’t change after.
- Every passenger or 100 kg delivered counts towards the goal everyone shares. Cargo events show it all in tonnes: the goal, the standings and what each job added. An event job carries at most 150 passengers, whatever the plane.
- Each tier the goal reaches pays everyone who helped, in a company or not. The rewards add up: every tier pays its own amount on top of the ones before, so the last column is what you’ve earned in all once that tier is reached.

| Tier | Of the goal | Pays | In all |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25% | $5,000 | $5,000 |
| 2 | 50% | $15,000 | $20,000 |
| 3 | 100% | $40,000 | $60,000 |
| 4 | 150% | $10,000 | $70,000 |
| 5 | 200% | $10,000 | $80,000 |

Past the goal, the stretch tiers keep paying for a big week.

The three companies that have delivered the most when the goal’s reached win trophies: the standings lock then, the race is over and the trophies are given at once, so nobody has to stay on the event all week. A week whose goal isn’t reached gives them at its end. Deliveries after still count for the stretch tiers. An exact tie goes to whoever got there first: the company (or pilot) whose last delivery came earlier, and for fields held, the company that took its latest field first.


#### Inside, Into and Out
Each event has a way its work goes, by its story, shown on the World screen:

- Inside: jobs start and end in the region, as above. Island chains, coasts, tours, homecomings across a country, the bush.
- Into: its jobs are on boards outside the region, as far as your plane reaches and up to 900 nm off, two a board, flying in from 100 nm or more. Boards inside have none: fly other work out and come back in. Festivals, races, ski fields, parks, relief and heavy lift.
- Out: its jobs are on boards inside the region, three a board, flying out to fields beyond it, 100 to 900 nm off, the nearer ones likelier. Each load counts towards holding the field it left, not where it lands, so the fields-held trophy is won by shipping out of them. The catch and the harvest.


#### Regions that grow, and other kinds
- A region grows with the pilots who come: about one field for every 10 pilots the last events drew, 25 at least. The place the event is about stays outlined inside it on the map, and deliveries into it count double. Boards in the wider ring have event jobs of their own.
- Some events move: the whale migration down the Pacific coast, the cherry blossom up Japan, a stage race round France. The week goes in legs, a day or two each, and the jobs follow the leg the event is on. The World screen says which leg it is and when the next starts.
- Some are paired: one theme in several places at once, like the Azores, Madeira and the Canaries, sharing one goal. Each place has its own event jobs, within it: none go from one place to another.
- Some have a rule: bush strips only (fields under 1,000 m), or heavy lift (loads of 3 t or more: the Shire, Zephyr, Heron, Pelican and Skyliner).
- The company holding the most fields in the region as the week ends wins a trophy of its own. The World screen shows who holds most.

Company › Pilots shows the week’s event for your own company: each of its pilots who has helped, what they’ve delivered and their place among everyone helping, and the company’s total.

A company’s trophies are listed on its page, and a gold cup with how many it has shows by its name on the leaderboards.

The World screen (the World button) shows the progress, how much you’ve delivered and your place among everyone helping, and the companies and pilots doing most (each pilot with their company’s tag).

Which event comes next is a surprise until the last 24 hours of the week: then the World screen says what and where it is (Preview its region on the map goes there), and the map outlines its region in blue as the next event. There are 235, on all 7 continents, each in its own season (south of the equator, the southern one). The next is always on another continent, and one that’s been on doesn’t come round again for 26 weeks.


### Landmark patrons

_Your name under a landmark, till someone pays more_

Pay to be a landmark’s patron and your call sign (or your company’s name) shows under it for everyone: on the map, beside it in the air, on its tours and photo jobs, and on postcards taken there. Anyone can take it over by paying more. It earns nothing: it’s for the glory.

- **To be its first patron:** $250,000 (the world’s most famous ones $1M)
- **To take one over:** 20% more than its patron paid
- **…falling:** 5% for every day they’ve held it, never below where it starts
- **To hold it longer:** Pay up to 3× its price: others then need 20% more than that, and each doubling is about two more weeks before it’s back to its start
- **Paid from:** Your money, or the company treasury: Captains (up to $250,000 a day) and the Chief Pilot

Tap a landmark on the map (gold ones have a patron; zoom in for the rest) to see its patron, what it costs now and who held it before, with their dates (with a trip picked, its card offers a fly-past first: tap Be its patron, or Patron: and their name, under it). Pick its price, or more to hold it longer (the card says what others will then need, and the day that’s fallen back to its start), and tap the button twice to pay. Live view on its card watches it from the air, as you would an airport: drag to turn round it and tilt, pinch to zoom, with whoever’s flying by tagged; × goes back to the map.

Taken over: you’re told at your next visit (a company also in its log). Nothing is paid back. A career deleted, or a company wound up, gives its landmarks up.

The landmarks you and your company are patron of are listed on your Career screen (Pilot tab), with what was paid and since when; tap one to see it on the map.

_No one’s name goes on Pearl Harbor, Uluru, Kata Tjuta and Arlington National Cemetery._


### World explored

_How much of the Earth you’ve flown over_

Every flight explores the land and sea within 20 km either side of its way, from where it set out (by the sight, on a tour) to where it lands. It counts when you land.

The Earth is split into 259,200 squares of 0.5° by 0.5°, and each counts once, by its true size: squares near the poles are smaller.

The World screen (the World button) shows the share of the Earth’s surface you’ve explored, and how much every pilot has explored together.

_Exploring started counting in October 2026: flights before then aren’t in it._

## Account and options

_Sync code, devices, settings_

### Sign-in and sync code

_Your career on any device_

Your career (money, planes, licenses, logbook) is kept safe on the game’s server. A new career starts with its first flight, then Sign in with Apple or Google keeps it (Your first flight says how): one career for each Apple or Google account.

- On another device, sign in with the same account: your career comes back there, a flight under way too.
- A career from before sign-ins: sign in from its pop-up or Menu → Options → Account, and from then on it comes back the same way.
- Which account it is: Menu → Options → Account shows its email, as Apple or Google gave it (Hide My Email gives a private relay address). The email is kept on that device, not on the server. Signed in before the game showed it, or on another device: “Sign in again” there shows it.
- A career with a sign-in comes to another device by signing in there: its sync code isn’t shown, and can’t be entered on another device. Steam’s sign-in is only in the Steam app: to play a Steam career on a phone or the web too, add Google or Apple to it first (Menu → Options → Account, in the Steam app), then sign in with that there. A career not signed in yet has one: Menu → Options → Account, hidden as dots till you tap it, so screenshots and videos don’t show it, then tap again to copy; on another device, Enter a sync code there or on the first screen (its own career is replaced). Anyone with the code can play that career, so keep it private.
- Signing in on a career with an account that’s on another of your careers: whichever has flown more keeps the sign-in and is the one you play. The other keeps its sync code. Signed in during a flight, the other career comes once that flight has landed.
- One flight at a time on all your devices: open the game on another and it picks up the flight under way. A device left in a flight that has since ended drops it, and its old landing isn’t paid. One whose copy of the flight has fallen behind the server’s clock picks it up where it really is.
- A take-off the server turns down (the job gone, another flight under way…) puts the plane back on the apron and says why: “Take-off refused: …”. One that got through but whose answer was lost on a weak connection is sent again and simply flies on: the server knows it as the same take-off.

_Until a career is signed in, the game asks now and then._


#### Deleting a career
Menu → Options → Account → Delete this career, with your call sign typed out to be sure. It’s deleted from the server at once, on every device, for good: its sign-in, flights, planes, place in a company (the last pilot out winds the company up) and postcards. Cancel a Pilots Club first, so it stops renewing. Purchases aren’t refunded or kept for another career. A beta pilot’s thank-you (the Bentwing) comes again to their next career if the deleted one never put money into a company.


### Beta thank-you

_For everyone who flew the beta_

Everyone who flies the beta gets a thank-you, once: a Bentwing, the gull-winged Navy fighter, $400,000 and 10 jumps. The plane is parked where you are when it comes (on the ground: one in the air waits till you land), and a card pops up to say thank you and show what’s in it.

The Bentwing is never sold: nobody else can ever have one, and you can’t sell it, so it’s never lost.


#### In the full game
Beta pilots get the same thank-you in the full game too. It knows you by your Apple or Google sign-in: your beta career (made before the full game opened, with a flight flown) signed in with the same account as your career in the full game. Signed in to the beta only after starting the full game? It still comes, within 24 hours, for 90 days after the full game opens. Each beta career brings one thank-you, to one career in the full game, whichever of its sign-ins you use.


### Your pilot

_Callsign and home airport_

Your callsign is your name in the sky: on the map, on tags beside your plane and in companies. In a company, other pilots see its tag after your callsign, on the map and over your plane in their view (Ace (BENT)). Change it on the Career screen (Pilot tab, Rename). Call signs are one pilot’s each: one another pilot has is taken (pilots who shared one before November 3, 2026 keep it), and one you change from is free for others.

Your home starts where your career began. It doesn’t tie you down: fly wherever the work is. Move it whenever you like, for free, to any airport you’re on the ground at: Make … my home at the top of the airport screen’s Hangar tab (or on the field’s card: tap the airport’s code, the button at the top left of the airport screen). The map marks it with a gold house, and the airport screen says Home.

_Names others see (callsigns, plane names, companies and tags) are checked for rude words, other pilots can report a callsign, and NimbleBit support can change one that’s hurtful (NimbleBit support, in this wiki)._


### Extra careers

_Alts: what’s fine and what isn’t_

Each career needs its own Apple or Google sign-in. A couple of extra careers for your own fun is fine: flying another plane somewhere else, or a formation for photos.


#### Not allowed
- More than one of your careers in the same company.
- Using careers to move money or planes to another career (through a company or any other way).
- Farming gifts or invite codes.
- Boosting a company, an airport’s holder, a world event or a leaderboard with extra careers.
- Automating a career (bots or scripts), even one.

A company wound up pays its last pilot only what they put in and their own flying earned it, never what others put in.

When several members of a company play from the same internet connection (half the company or more) and look like one player: some of their careers started at about the same time in the same browser, or they keep paying in or taking off from different airports within a minute or two of each other, the first of them to join is as usual and the others are on hold till support has looked: they can’t pay into it, and their flying gives it no share and doesn’t count towards its airports, events or building projects. They still fly anything and keep all they earn. Separate players on their own phones or computers aren’t held, even on one connection. If you’re held and you’re separate players, ask in #pilots-support on our Discord (Menu → Discord) and support will clear it.

_NimbleBit support may take back what was gained, ground or remove careers, or ban, as the terms say. Sharing a connection alone is never held against you beyond that pause._


### NimbleBit support

_Help, messages, names, grounding, bans_

Something wrong with your career (a purchase that didn’t arrive, a flight that won’t end, a plane stuck somewhere)? Ask in #pilots-support on our Discord (Menu → Discord) with your callsign: support can look your career up and put it right.


#### Messages
A message from support, to you or to everyone, pops up as a card the next time you play, once, or is said on the radio, once: the controller calls you by your call sign (or calls all stations) in flight, captioned, the next time you have the radio voices on (with them off, it’s a caption). When support changes something on your career, a line says so.


#### Names others see
Callsigns, plane names, companies and tags are for everyone to see. Support can change one that’s rude or hurtful, and block a word from new names.


#### Reporting a callsign
See a callsign that’s rude, hateful or sexual? Report it: the flag on a pilot’s card on the map or beside their rank on your company’s Pilots page, or tap their tag in an airport’s live view or their row on a Top pilots or event table. A card asks you to be sure. You can report each pilot once a day; nobody is told who reported them.

Reports go to NimbleBit support, the most reported first. Once 3 pilots have reported a callsign (pilots in one company count as one), everyone sees a plain one like N123AB instead till support has looked. Support then gives it back, if it’s fine, or resets it for good. If it’s yours, a line tells you at your next visit; you can pick another callsign meanwhile, but not the reported one again.


#### Grounding
A career that breaks the rules can be grounded for a while: till then it can’t take off, rename anything, found or join a company, or share postcards. Everything else stays: your planes, money and company. A card says till when, and a take-off says so too.


#### Banning
For the worst breaks of the rules, support can ban a career: nothing it does goes through, its planes leave the map, and its Apple or Google sign-in can’t start another. A card says so, with how to ask support to look again. You can still delete a banned career.

### The real world

_Where the world comes from_

Everything you fly over is built from real data, as it is today:

- Terrain from elevation surveys; forests, fields, towns and water from satellite land cover.
- Real airports and their runways; at bigger ones, taxiways and aprons from OpenStreetMap.
- Highways, and city buildings at their real heights.
- Marinas, with boats moored along their real pontoons (OpenStreetMap). Boats out on the bays, harbours and big lakes by the real weather: most on sunny, warm, calm summer weekends, few in rain, fog or strong wind, hardly any in a gale or by night, and yachts sail only in a breeze. The marinas empty as the bays fill. Ships at sea whatever the weather, busiest near the big ports (the World Port Index), lying at anchor off them with their bows to the wind.
- Today’s weather from airport reports, and rain from satellites.
- Real wildfires NASA’s satellites spotted in the last day.
- Crop dusting over the real farmland round small fields.
- The sun, moon and stars where they really are, and local time everywhere.
- The northern and southern lights where they are tonight, from NOAA’s space weather model.

_The sources are named at the foot of the menu._
